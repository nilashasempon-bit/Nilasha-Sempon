Hook: the sports day mystery

Sports day at Zayed Sports City…

Saif stands at the far end of the track, 340 m from the starter. He sees the starter's flag drop, then about one second later he hears BANG!

Hamdan is right next to him, with his ear pressed against the long metal railing. He hears the bang almost instantly.

Same bang, same distance. So why did Hamdan hear it first?

Predict: what is different about the path the sound took to Saif and to Hamdan? Talk with your partner for 1 minute.
Clue: sound passes energy from particle to particle. How close are the particles in air, and in metal?

Encounter: gas, liquid or solid?

What is the sound mainly travelling through? Tap each situation: GAS → LIQUID → SOLID. Then rank: which medium carries sound fastest?

Ranking: sound travels fastest through solids, then liquids, and slowest through gases. In a solid the particles are packed tightly, so they collide and pass the energy on very quickly.

Speed and diffraction (the unconventional version)

Think of it like this

Pass a message along a line of students. If everyone stands shoulder to shoulder (a solid), the message flies along. If everyone stands far apart (a gas), it takes much longer. And when the message reaches an open door, it spreads out into the next room: that is diffraction.

Textbook ideas

Two factors change the speed of sound: the density and the temperature of the medium.
Diffraction is the change in direction of a wave when it travels by the edge of an object or through an opening.

Q1 A car horn is heard almost immediately, but a beach wave takes a while to reach the shore. What does this tell you?
✓ Different waves travel at different speeds. Sound in air is fast (about 343 m/s); water waves at the beach are much slower.
Q2 What would happen if you placed something between you and a microphone in a recording studio?
✓ Some sound would reflect off it or be absorbed, so less would reach the microphone. Some might also diffract around its edges.

Textbook: speed of sound

From your textbook
Read, then answer
Q1 In the table, where does sound travel fastest, and where slowest?
✓ Fastest in glass (5,640 m/s). Slowest in air at 0°C (331 m/s).
Q2 Why does sound travel faster in a solid than in a gas?
✓ In a solid the particles are packed very close together, so they collide and transfer energy very quickly.
Q3 How does temperature change the speed of sound in air? And in water?
✓ Warmer air: faster (343 m/s at 20°C vs 331 at 0°C). Liquids are the opposite: cooler water is faster (1,500 m/s at 0°C vs 1,481 at 20°C).

Textbook: diffraction

From your textbook
Read, then answer
Q1 What is diffraction? Give an everyday example.
✓ The change in direction of a wave when it travels by the edge of an object or through an opening. Example: hearing people talking in a room before you reach its open door.
Q2 When does the most diffraction happen?
✓ When the size of the object or opening is similar to the wavelength of the wave.
Remember: both water waves and sound waves diffract.

Textbook: life science connection

From your textbook
Read, then answer
Q1 Name the three main parts of the ear.
✓ The outer ear, the middle ear and the inner ear.
Q2 Put these in the order sound reaches them: cochlea, ear canal, hammer–anvil–stirrup, eardrum.
✓ Ear canal → eardrum → hammer, anvil, stirrup → cochlea.
Big idea: hearing is an interaction of waves with matter. Sound travels through air, then solid parts, then the liquid in the cochlea.

Sound speed race: 1 km through each medium

Each lane sends the same sound 1,000 m through a different medium, using the speeds in your textbook table. Predict the order, then press Race!

time = distance ÷ speed
The solids (glass, iron, ice) win easily, liquids come next, and air is slowest. Sound crosses 1 km of glass in about 0.18 s, but needs about 2.9 s in air.

Temperature and the speed of sound in air

As a gas gets warmer, its particles move faster and collide more often, so sound travels faster. A good rule for air is:

speed ≈ 331 + 0.6 × temperature (°C)

Thunder over Hatta

You see lightning, then hear thunder 6 s later. How far away is the storm?

✓ At 20°C: 6 × 343 ≈ 2,058 m, about 2 km away. (We see the lightning first because light travels far faster than sound.)
Air particles at this temperature
Faster particles collide more often and pass the sound on sooner.

Diffraction: waves through a gap

Waves arrive from the left and pass through a gap in a barrier. More diffraction happens when the gap is similar in size to the wavelength.

Sound waves have wavelengths of centimetres to metres, similar to the size of a doorway, so they diffract a lot. Light waves are far smaller than a doorway, so they hardly bend at all.

Breaking Waves lab: waves through gaps

You need: a shallow tray of water · a wooden dowel or ruler · two blocks · a small object (e.g. a stone)
  1. Place the two blocks across the tray with a wide gap between them.
  2. Wave-maker: gently roll the dowel once to make a straight wave.
  3. Observer: watch the wave after it passes through the gap.
  4. Repeat with a narrow gap, then with a small object instead of the blocks. Record below.
Set-upWhat did the wave do after the gap or object?
Wide gap
Mostly carried straight on; it spread out only a little at the edges.
Narrow gap
Spread out in curved, circular waves: lots of diffraction.
Small object
The wave bent around the edges and joined up again behind the object.
Safety: keep water away from electrical sockets and wipe up spills straight away.
Roles
  • Wave-maker: makes one gentle wave at a time.
  • Gap-keeper: sets the blocks to the right gap.
  • Observer and recorder: describes and records what the wave does.
The waves didn't break: they changed direction at the barrier. The narrower the gap (closer to the wavelength), the more the waves spread out. That is diffraction.

Why Hamdan heard it first
