Encounter: reflect, absorb or transmit?

Tap a situation to sort it. Each tap moves it on: REFLECT → ABSORB → TRANSMIT. Then check with your partner.

Why? Hard, smooth surfaces mostly reflect waves. Soft, porous materials mostly absorb the energy. Thin materials let some energy transmit (pass through).
Remember: most real surfaces do a bit of all three. We sort by what happens most.

Three things a wave can do (the unconventional version)

Think of it like this

Throw a football at a concrete wall: it bounces back (reflection). Throw it into a soft sand dune at Liwa: it stops, and its energy goes into the sand (absorption). Kick it at a loose curtain: it pushes through to the other side, slower (transmission). Waves do all three!

Textbook definitions

Reflection is the bouncing of a wave off a surface.
Absorption is the transfer of energy by a wave to the medium through which it travels.
Transmission is the passage of a wave through a medium.

Angle of reflection: angle in = angle out

In the lab Crashing Waves, you changed the angle of the barrier and the wave bounced off in a new direction. Scientists measure both angles from the normal: an imaginary line at 90° to the barrier.

Predict the angle out, then send the wave.
Top view of a wave tank
Whatever angle you choose, the reflected wave leaves at exactly the same angle on the other side of the normal. The wave changes direction, but the angles stay equal.

Echoes at Hatta Dam

An echo is a reflected sound wave. Sound travels at about 343 m/s in air. The sound must go to the cliff and back, so:

echo time = 2 × distance ÷ 343

Predict: how long until you hear the echo?
The walls are so close that the reflection comes back almost instantly and mixes with your voice. Your brain hears a separate echo only when the cliff is roughly 17 m or more away (about 0.1 s delay).

Highway sound barriers: how high?

Traffic noise on a busy road can be about 75 dB. A hard barrier reflects the sound back towards the road. The taller the barrier, the more sound it reflects away from the homes.

Noise levels are example values to compare heights.
Foam would absorb some sound, but it is weak outdoors and wears away in sun, rain and dust. Concrete, wood and metal are strong and reflect the noise back towards the road.

Crashing Waves lab: barriers and waves

You need: a shallow tray of water · a wooden dowel or ruler · a block (barrier) · a sponge or folded towel · a protractor
  1. Place the block across one end of the tray as a barrier.
  2. Wave-maker: gently roll the dowel once to make a straight wave.
  3. Observer: watch what the wave does when it hits the barrier.
  4. Turn the barrier to an angle and repeat. Then cover the barrier with the sponge and repeat. Record below.
BarrierWhat happened to the wave?
Straight block
Reflected straight back towards you.
Block at an angle
Reflected in a new direction, at the same angle it hit the block.
Block covered with sponge
A much smaller reflection: the sponge absorbed most of the energy.
Safety: keep water away from electrical sockets and wipe up spills straight away.
Roles
  • Wave-maker: makes one gentle wave at a time.
  • Observer: watches the barrier closely and describes the wave.
  • Recorder: writes the results in the table.
Hard barriers reflect the wave, at the same angle it arrives. Soft barriers absorb energy, so the reflected wave is smaller. If you see a small wave behind the barrier, some energy was transmitted.

Where the echo went

In the empty majlis, Hamdan's voice hit hard, bare walls that REFLECTED most of the sound energy back to him as an echo. The carpets, cushions and curtains are soft and porous, so they ABSORBED much of the sound ENERGY. With less energy reflected, the echo became too weak to hear!
