The pattern: radio waves, microwaves and infrared have longer wavelengths than visible light. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays have shorter wavelengths. Our eyes can only see the narrow band in the middle: visible light.

Light and the spectrum (the unconventional version)

Think of it like this

Imagine a family of seven siblings who all travel at the same speed. They look different because their wavelengths are different. The eldest, radio, takes huge, lazy steps. The youngest, gamma, takes tiny, very fast steps and carries the most energy. Our eyes can only see one sibling in the middle: visible light.

Textbook ideas

Light in nanometres: find the colour

Light waves are tiny! We measure them in nanometres: 1 nm = 0.000 000 001 m, one-billionth of a metre. Visible light runs from about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet).

A water wave with a bigger amplitude has more energy. But the energy of an electromagnetic wave depends on its frequency, not its amplitude: higher frequency = more energy. That is why ultraviolet can burn and gamma rays can damage cells.

Rainbow lab: split light, then find the invisible

You need: a clear glass of water · a sheet of white paper · a torch (or sunlight from a window) · a TV remote · a phone camera
  1. Part A: stand the glass of water at the edge of a desk, on the white paper.
  2. Shine the torch (or let sunlight) through the water at an angle. Move the paper until you see a band of colours.
  3. Record the colours from top to bottom.
  4. Part B: point a TV remote at a phone camera and press a button. Look with your eyes, then look at the phone screen.
TestWhat did you observe?
Colours on the paper
A small rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet. White light is a mix of all the colours.
Remote: with your eyes
Nothing! The remote's infrared light is invisible to our eyes.
Remote: on the phone screen
A flashing light. Many phone cameras can detect infrared, which our eyes cannot.
Safety: never look directly at the Sun or shine a torch into anyone's eyes. Keep water away from sockets and wipe up spills straight away.
Roles
  • Light-holder: holds the torch steady at an angle.
  • Observer: moves the paper and describes the colours.
  • Recorder: writes the observations in the table.
White light is made of all the colours of visible light. Each colour has a different wavelength. The remote's infrared is an electromagnetic wave too: it is there, but its wavelength is too long for our eyes.
Part A · How the glass of water makes a rainbow
  1. White light from the torch or the Sun is really a mix of all the colours, each with its own wavelength (red ≈ 700 nm … violet ≈ 400 nm).
  2. When light passes from air into water (and out again), it slows down and bends. This bending is called refraction.
  3. Each colour bends by a slightly different amount: violet bends the most, red bends the least. So the colours spread out into a band: a spectrum.
  4. Rainbows in the sky (and in the spray at Hatta Dam) happen the same way: sunlight is split by millions of tiny water droplets.
Part B · Why the phone camera sees the remote's light
  1. The remote sends its signal as infrared flashes. Infrared has a longer wavelength than red light (about 940 nm), so our eyes cannot detect it.
  2. A phone camera's sensor can detect some infrared. The phone shows it as visible light on the screen, so you see the flashes!
Big idea: light we can see is only one part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Our eyes have limits, but science and technology help us “see” the invisible.

Why Saif could only see the rainbow

The warmth, the rainbow and the sunburn were all ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES from the Sun. The warmth was infrared, with wavelengths longer than light. The sunburn came from ultraviolet, with wavelengths shorter than light. Saif's eyes can only detect VISIBLE LIGHT, about 700–400 nm, so he only saw the rainbow. Ultraviolet has a higher frequency, so it carries more energy: enough to burn his skin. Time for sunscreen!
